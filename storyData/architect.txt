It was only a couple thousand years after the Sun burned out, scorching Earth, the origin planet of humans, that the species achieved absolute control. Humans have always wanted power, from the days they hit each other with sticks to become village leaders, to the great AI-enforced world dictatorships of semi-ancient Earth, all the way to the galaxy-wide war for the practically infinite space of the universe.
That was when a group of people decided on a way of fixing things. They realized that the fewer people there are, the fewer problems arise. With an entire universe full of people, there are many with malicious intent, and at that scale, no one is willing to stand up for themselves. But in a small group of maybe only twenty people, if one person rises to power and is unjust, they can be easily overthrown. The places without problems were the small villages on remote planets with fewer than one hundred people.
Taking inspiration from this, the Final Gods devised a powerful mechanism. It would tear the universe apart, leaving it in tiny fragments, often just individual star systems. Everybody would live in these secluded star systems, and they would be controlled and changed over time by an architect. Still, there came the problem of picking an architect.

I was only twelve when the Great Fracture split the universe. Too naïve to be scared of the cosmic powers that created the Fracture, I was more worried about my friends: the ones I would lose because they were on the other side of the Fracture, and the ones I would make because I was stuck. Back then, I felt so trapped, only being able to exist within one star system.
When you have been living in a universe at a scale beyond any number we can give a name to, you overlook large things and take them for insignificant and small. This is how I had become so accustomed to immense scales that even some galaxies were puny to me, the star systems in them numbering only in the thousands.
Being confined to a single star system, though, I realized how immense it truly was. The star system I had been trapped in had three planets that had been pushed into habitable orbits and nurtured into life. Each one of them ranged from cold polar ice caps to lush forests to oceans, from sprawling farmland to dense cities. Over the years, I became accustomed to even the size of planets being enormous.
We moved to a town close enough to the farmland that we'd have plentiful food, but industrial enough to have a spaceport, allowing us to travel almost anywhere. I began to make friends, lots of them. By the time I was fifteen, I knew almost every one of the 347 people in the town. It was nice being somewhere permanent, somewhere in such a small place in space and time.
Then one day I got sick. The village doctor quickly identified it and told me it was incurable. I made him keep it a secret. I didn't want to upset all my friends and everybody I knew. Instead, I told them that I had found a good job opportunity on one of the other planets. They were sad that I was leaving, but nowhere near as sad as they would have been had I told them I was dying.
I died on the transfer ship.
When I opened my eyes again, I was not on the ship anymore. I was floating in some sort of black void. Looking around, I noticed I didn't have a body of any kind. I was simply perceiving this void somehow. Then I noticed a blue ball, slightly shimmering and floating about a foot away from me. A questioning algorithm.
"What is this place?" I asked.
Questioning algorithms are computer programs created to replicate conscious intelligence. Kind of. They're specifically designed to be more robotic. Their job is to analyze a data set they've been given and answer any questions they're asked, as opposed to replicating an actual human being's personality.
"This is an architect world."
"What do you mean by that?"
"You are an architect. This is your world."
"What does it mean for me to be an architect?"
"You will tweak your native star system to make it better for everybody alive in it. You will prevent extinction and large-scale monarchies or dictatorships."
"So I'm like the God of my star system?"
"Yes."
"Why me? How do you pick?"
"Every person alive in the star system who dies within the first one hundred years is a candidate. Whoever personally and individually improves the lives of the people connected to them within two degrees of separation will be picked. If this person declines, it will go to the next person down."
"I can decline? How many other people declined before me?"
"Only three. Would you like to decline?"
"No, I don't. I'm gonna be an architect."

For the first little while, it was pretty easy. People were still settling down and often took care of their own power problems, just as the Final Gods had predicted. It was later that I began to struggle.
People were getting smarter in the ways they tried to rise to power, and it was hard looking everywhere in the star system at once. I was picked because humanity and each individual were in my best interest, not because of my skill. Often, malicious people would gain a pretty good following before I had to intervene. Intervening was also difficult because I wanted to do it in a way that didn't actually kill the person, although I did have to do that once or twice. Usually, I can find a way to prevent their monarchy or dictatorship, but it's definitely hard without making it seem unnatural. It's been almost one thousand years, and the population is continuing to grow and expand.
That's where you come in. You're dying soon, you've had a diverse range of experiences, you're open to new ways of doing things, and you have every individual in your best interest. If this goes well, I might even expand into a group of many people. I will, of course, give you more information should you choose to say yes, but I believe this covers the basics.
