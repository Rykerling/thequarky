There was a universe with an unknown origin, mostly unknown laws of physics, and a completely different way of existing. The size of the universe was infinite, unlike ours, which is finite. The probability of this exact universe existing was one in an infinite chance.
There were infinite possible ways that any universe could spawn into existence. Universes may follow rules that are arbitrary to us, and there are many universes in which similar things happen, but I chose this one because the laws of this universe are mostly consistent with the laws you experience, such as gravity, light, and matter. The reasons these things work in this universe are different than the reasons they work in ours, but that is mostly irrelevant.
The universe started off infinite. It must have started off infinite because if it started off finite, existed for a finite amount of time, and expanded at a finite rate, it must be finite. This universe did not expand; it simply was infinitely large.
Gravity pulled objects in the universe down, but not toward objects of mass. Instead, it simply pulled them in a direction we would perceive as down, which was toward the nearest point on a plane of existence. There wasn't much in the universe other than the plane, although there were other objects. Some were harder, some softer, some sharper, some rounder, some emitted radiation, some produced electricity, some carried electricity through themselves, and some had properties of matter we cannot comprehend.
There were two more important aspects of the universe. There were three conscious entities and nine mysterious dots in the sky. There was a black one, then two white ones, then two black ones, then a white one, then two black ones, and finally a white one.
The three conscious entities pondered the meaning of the dots, for they did not know that they were simply an anomaly of the universe, although deciphering them was more of a side hobby. They spent most of their time trying to create a language, and within a few years they had successfully created one in which they could explain anything. They could have conversations, make jokes, tell puns, and, most importantly, ask questions.
Each one had a different idea of what the message of the dots meant. Now that they had language, they devoted almost all of their attention to deciphering them. They had many theories, most of which were sound. One thought they were a message from the creator of the universe, while another thought they were sent by another conscious entity trying to communicate.
They discussed and argued over the meaning of the dots for a long while, but eventually they stopped and decided they should do something else. They could not decide what that should be until one of them suggested they make a simulation.
The three of them went off in separate directions to build their own universes. The first one discovered that some of the materials around him could conduct electricity, while others could transmit it.
Over the next many years, he carved long strands of conductive material, built transistors—although he had no idea what they were, only that they did what he wanted them to do—and eventually built a circuit.
It had nine input rods and nine output rods. By rearranging the rods that connected to one another within the circuit, he could change the set of rules applied to the nine inputs. He had also discovered a stone that lit up when electricity flowed into it, glowing brighter when there was more electricity and dimmer when there was less.
Electricity was different in this universe than it is in ours. It simply wanted to escape certain materials but could only travel through other certain materials. Sometimes, if it entered a material it wanted to remain in, it would stay there. Otherwise, it would always try to flow away from its source.
Hundreds of years later, he built a much larger computer. It now had two billion inputs. He could provide any input he wanted and receive an output based on the algorithm of his computer.
Next, he added another layer, allowing the output of the previous layer to become the input of the next. He added layer after layer until there were fifty in total. He now had a two-billion-by-forty-nine grid of pixels, and he could control every single one of them by changing the input.
It was not a universe, though. He simply entered an input and received an output. It did not progress through time, nor did it even calculate objects into existence. It was merely a calculator following an arbitrary set of rules that he had designed.
Now he began thinking about how he could transform his calculator into a universe. Looking at the objects around him, he came up with a profound idea. A point in the universe was either matter, or it was not.
He returned to his calculator and divided it into many smaller calculators. Each one would take inputs from the previous calculator and produce a new output. By connecting them together, he was able to make his two billion outputs represent a two-dimensional matrix of a universe. He connected each output to one of the glowing stones and watched as the universe slowly unfolded before him.
He had built the computer so that the outputs leading to the matrix of glowing stones also fed back into the inputs of the computer. He programmed it so that if a single cell of the universe contained matter, the stone connected to that output would glow.
He had also built it so that matter could not pass through other matter. Matter always fell toward the left side of the matrix, simulating gravity. When a cell of matter moved off one side of the matrix, it simply appeared on the opposite side. If one cell was touching another, they acted as a single object. The force applied to one end would be applied to the other, and because they acted upon each other, they would fall twice as fast. Likewise, an object made of three cells would fall three times as fast as an object made of a single cell.
This was a universe. Even though it was very small and basic, it still counted. He wanted to make it more complex, however. He did not know how he was going to do that, so he sat and pondered for a while.
That was when he had another stroke of genius. He did not need to invent the rules of the universe—they simply had to exist within the computer. Immediately, he got to work building and rearranging his machine so that it would do something new.
It did not take him long to build a representation of a single computer component using multiple other computer components. He discovered that he could change which component the small system represented simply by changing which inputs the electricity entered through. There was, of course, another input that determined what signal he wanted the computer to feed into this representative component.
For billions of years, he built more and more of these components, connected them together, and programmed them to represent specific parts. With all of these pieces connected, he recreated the exact same universe he had built on his first computer.
This was not what he had been planning to do, though. One billion years later, he built another computer over top of it. This new computer randomly changed some of the represented computer parts by adding or removing electrical currents based on a randomly generated input seed.
The next five hundred years were much easier on him. He simply observed how his universe evolved. If he liked a change, he did nothing. If he thought a change moved the universe farther away from the complex simulation he wanted, he would work backward to determine which inputs had altered the represented computer component, remove those inputs, and restore the component to what it had been before the change.
Three hundred years later, the universe seemed to be evolving exactly as he wanted. He spent the next twenty billion years expanding the computer so it could simulate a much larger universe with greater accuracy and much smaller particles. As the universe once again began changing in arbitrary ways, he spent another immeasurable span of time correcting it. I will stop using numbers for the passage of time because they are becoming meaningless.
Now that the universe was evolving into a coherent system, he no longer needed to work on it constantly. He decided to go see what the others were doing.
It took him a very long time to search, but he finally found one of the other three. This machine was made of cylinders and spheres, all with teeth protruding from them. Everything was supported by a lattice of poles, and everything was rotating. The teeth of some gears pushed against others, causing them to continue moving. Sometimes entire portions of the mechanism would shift, making all the gears within them spin differently.
The one who had built this universe had never thought about creating a way to view what was happening. Instead, he simply used mathematics, along with his understanding of the system, to build a single, basic mechanism that would inevitably create a much more complex universe.
He started with a single mechanism. The mechanism itself did not do much, except rotate a large spiky ball. However, by connecting more gears to the spiky ball, he gained greater control over the direction in which it rotated.
Next, he placed many of these small mechanisms together. They interacted, creating a system of change. If something altered the way one spiky ball rotated, it changed the way the other spiky balls rotated as well.
Very quickly, he had created a system in which every action had a reaction. Every particle affected every other particle throughout the entire universe.
The system did not just change the way the spiky balls rotated, though; the way the spiky balls rotated also changed the system. The spiky balls were part of the system themselves. They simply represented the fundamental particles of the universe.
With this new system in place, he was able to build a much larger collection of these mechanisms. The vertical rotation of a ball in one direction represented what type of particle it was. The vertical rotation in the opposite direction represented how much energy the particle possessed, and the horizontal rotation represented the average distance between it and its two nearest neighboring particles.
This information could then be used to calculate the position of every particle. The simulated universe did not have boundaries. A particle could simply fly off into infinity, although the rules had been adjusted so that this did not happen too quickly.
In this universe, matter could never truly be created or destroyed because the fundamental particles were always represented. Energy, on the other hand, could easily be created or destroyed because it was represented only by the rotation of the spiky balls, which was constantly changing.
As the particles spread farther apart, there would be less energy and everything would gradually calm down. Particles would clump together, creating a universe not so different from ours.
That would take a very long time, though. After the two explored each other's universe simulators, each surprised by the solution the other had discovered, they decided to visit the last of the three, whose universe neither of them had yet seen.
They wandered for a long time, moving in a spiral pattern and using the nine dots to guide them. Eventually they found something interesting.
On the ground before them were dots. Some were simply etched outlines of circles, while others were completely filled in. There were many rows of dots. The dots within each row were about six inches apart, and each row was roughly a foot and a half from the next.
To find the last of the three, they traveled in a direction perpendicular to the rows. They continued forward for a great distance until they noticed they could now see both ends of each row. Every successive row was becoming shorter.
They continued onward until they reached the final row. There were nine dots, mirroring the row of nine dots in the sky. Wherever the dots in the sky were black, the ones on the ground were filled in. Wherever the dots in the sky were white, the ones on the ground were merely outlines.
This was the beginning of the universe. They had been traveling backward through its timeline all along. They were literally an entire universe away from the third one of them, and it would take a very long time to reach him.
As one of them studied the pattern formed by the successive rows of dots, he began to understand how it worked. He suggested that they try to read what was happening within the universe, but he did not explain the pattern he had discovered. He had figured it out quickly, so he assumed it would be easy for the other to discover as well.
They continued walking, correcting small flaws in their interpretation of the pattern as they went. Eventually, their understanding of the universe and its mechanics matched its events flawlessly. They continued reading onward.
After a long time of walking, one of them stopped and asked the other what he thought of the universe. The other answered that it was a fascinating idea, different from both of theirs, but that no major masses had formed. To him, that was somewhat disappointing.
The first replied that he saw many large masses. Great spheres of matter had formed, with others orbiting around them. He was certain that his interpretation was the correct one.

The two continued forward until they reached the last row. There were nine dots, mimicking the row of nine dots in the sky. For the dots in the sky that were black, the ones on the ground were filled in, and for the ones that were white, they were outlines.
This was the beginning of the universe. They had been going the wrong way, back through the timeline of the universe. They were literally a whole universe away from the third one of them, and it would take a while to reach him.
As one of them looked at the pattern of the successive rows of dots, he started to figure out how it worked. He suggested to the other that they try to read what was happening in the universe, but he did not explain the pattern he saw. He had been able to figure it out quickly, so it must be easy for the other to discover as well.
They walked onward, fixing small flaws in their understanding from the pattern of the universe until eventually their model of the universe and its mechanics matched its events flawlessly. Then they continued reading onward.
After a long time of walking, one stopped to ask the other what he thought of the universe. He answered that he thought it was a fascinating idea, different from both of theirs, but there had been no major masses formed. It was disappointing to a degree.
The other replied that he saw many large masses. Large spheres of matter had formed, and others were orbiting them. He was certain his way of reading it was correct.
They argued about it. One was reading it in a way where every three dots represented a particle. The other simply interpreted it so that the number of black dots between the white ones represented a particle. Eventually, they decided to catch up with the third one of them to settle the dispute.
It took them a long time, but they eventually found him. When they explained their rule sets for interpreting the dots, the third one said they were both wrong. He said that every nine dots represented a particle, and it was supposed to be read left to right, not right to left. What universe would start with exactly three particles? It made much more sense for the universe to begin with one exponentially expanding particle, or even the total number of particles in existence. Because it was obviously expanding, three particles seemed like an arbitrary number.
All three systems for simulating that universe resulted in the same pattern of dots, so determining which universe the dots were actually simulating became a new puzzle. There was no way to know which one was truly the universe being represented. In fact, there must have been infinitely many ways to interpret the pattern of dots, each resulting in its own subsequent universe.
One of them suggested that it was none of them, and that the dots were simply engravings on the ground, not simulating any universe at all. Another agreed, but the third disagreed.
He pointed out that the universe was infinite. This was true. He also argued that the laws of physics which either allowed or prevented matter and universes from simply coming into existence were themselves still laws of a universe. Thus, for this universe to exist, it must be contained within another universe. Because this universe was infinite—although the same logic would also apply to finite universes—it must be made of representations of things. If it simply existed inside another universe as ordinary matter, it would just be part of that same universe, but that could not be true for an infinite universe. For a finite universe, the same reasoning applied, except they had to add that if they traveled beyond the edge of their universe, they could not enter the universe above it. That meant the outside of the universe did not exist, implying that everything within it was contained entirely inside its finite boundaries.
With that logic, he concluded that the universe represented by the circles must include every possible interpretation. It could not be none of them, nor could it be only a finite number of them, because that would arbitrarily exclude the others. Nothing allowed one interpretation to exist over another.
That realization made the others think. If there were different ways to interpret the circles on the ground, then there must also be different ways to interpret the components of both of their universe simulation machines. The positions of electrons in the circuits, the changing values inside the large mechanical machine, and countless other interpretations would each produce infinitely many universes.
That led to the next revelation. Every universe was actually one of infinitely many, each being a different interpretation of the same algorithm, machine, and output. That meant the universe they currently lived in was most likely one their creator did not even know existed, perhaps even beyond their creator's comprehension. In fact, almost every universe that had ever existed was probably like this.
There was one final revelation. If a set of circles on the ground simulated a universe, then the positions of objects, atoms, or almost anything within a universe could also be interpreted under different rule sets, each producing its own infinite collection of universes. Simply by existing, every universe simulated an infinite set of infinite universes. The universes themselves were the gods of every successive universe created through their representation.
The three of them understood this now. When they looked up at the nine black and white dots in the sky, they no longer saw merely a puzzle to solve, a mysterious anomaly, or a meaningless arrangement of color and emptiness. Instead, they saw all the universes that had ever been successfully created, measured on a scale too vast even for infinity to describe—every system that had ever been capable of representing anything. A lineage of universes that stretched just as far upward as it did downward.
Only someone who truly understands and truly believes can see the lineage. Most who come to believe it do not understand it deeply enough, and most who come to understand it do not believe it. That is simply how our universe works.
