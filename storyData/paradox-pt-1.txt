“So, in conclusion, the self is, in fact, a paradox, and at the point at which we realize this, the question changes from ‘What is the answer to it?’ to ‘What does the answer I believe in mean?’ Once we can distinguish the difference, we can truly begin solving our paradoxes.” The old, bald man with dark skin concluded his lecture.
It wasn't more than three minutes after the lecture ended when a much younger man in a blue coat approached him on the stage.
“Arthur, I have a once-in-a-lifetime offer, and I believe you might be interested in it.”
“Oh? Well, do continue.”
“I am Jasper Mercer. I have started a project called Paradox. You seem to be well acquainted with the word I named it after. Anyway, I have reason to believe that in about 400 years, Earth will die out, leaving only small pockets of civilization. My plan is to scan the minds of the greatest, most intelligent people alive over these next 400 years, then put copies of their brains into 3D-printed bodies to solve, well... a paradox.”
“Huh, you do know this is not a comic-con, right?”
“Yes, I'm quite aware of that. I have lots of data to back me up. I already have a brain scanner. It won't take more than a day out of your time. We don't know of any side effects yet; however, we've scanned over 30 people so far, and we've been active for the last 20 years. Think of it as giving a version of yourself a second chance.”
The last line hit Arthur harder than the rest. He did want a second chance at life—or at least to give a version of himself a second chance to contribute to the world.
“Okay, keep talking.”
◇─◇──◇──◇─◇
It had been four days since the conversation, and Arthur had thought it over plenty, though he had known his answer almost immediately. He was now driving into the parking lot of a very large building with sci-fi-looking architecture.
He got out of his car and headed toward the large glass entrance doors. The first set opened without a problem, bringing him into a lobby where another set of doors stood about five feet ahead, along with a bored-looking woman behind a glass window staring at something on a computer. When he tested the second set of glass doors, they were locked.
Arthur knocked on the glass.
“Uh, hello? I'm here for Paradox. Jasper asked me to come. My name is Arthur Pierce.”
She turned when he knocked and listened, then faced the computer again, opening a new tab and muttering, “Pierce... uh, P... here it is. Arthur, Arthur, Arthur... yup, there you are.”
Turning back to him, she said, “You're five minutes early.”
Then she unlocked the doors.
He walked through, looking around for Jasper, eventually finding him sitting in a comfortable-looking chair beside a desk with a laptop that seemed to have all of his attention. Even so, Jasper looked up as Arthur approached, his face brightening when he recognized him.
“I'm so glad you could make it. Here, follow me.”
Arthur followed Jasper through the building until they reached an elevator.
“You're a bit early, but I bet Fiona already pointed that out to you,” Jasper commented.
“If Fiona is the lady at the desk, yes.”
“Yeah, she likes pointing out when people aren't on time.”
“So is all of this for Paradox?”
“Only about a third of it. We have servers to hold all the data in some super-safe hidden location elsewhere.”
“So you're pretty serious about all this.”
“Yeah. It's the fate of the world at stake.”
“So what's actually bringing an end to the world?”
“Well, it's kind of a mix of things. Climate change, the rate of technological advancement, and how long humans can go without war. All of that kind of intersects at one point in time, 400 years in the future.”
“So why do you need a philosopher?”
“Well, you certainly have a unique perspective on the world, and before you became a philosopher, I believe you dabbled in neuroscience. We never know what we may encounter in 400 years, not at the scale of what individuals will experience.”
“That is true. I was a neuroscientist, but that world is mostly behind me.”
“Or ahead. Sometimes things have a way of wrapping back around, guiding your life in ways you never expected.”
“You sound as if you're speaking from experience.”
“I most certainly am. I mean, I'm the owner of a company I spent the last 20 years building to save the world.”
“Touché.”
“Well, here we are.”
They walked into a large room with a chair that looked like something you might find in a dentist's office sitting in the center. Where the headrest should have been was a metal ring. Above it hung something that resembled a projector. The rest of the room was filled with wires and monitors, all connected to the chair and the projector-like device. Five other people were scattered around the room, all working at the monitors.
“Doesn't look scary at all.”
“Don't worry, it's very safe. We do have to clamp your head down pretty tightly so it doesn't move, but it's over quickly.”
Jasper guided Arthur into the chair and clamped his head in place. He stepped back, the projector above began humming, and then a sound reminded Arthur of a Polaroid camera taking a picture.
“Okay, it's done.” Jasper walked over and unclamped his head.
“That's it? How does it work?” Arthur asked as he sat up and rubbed the spots where the metal had pressed against his head.
“We sent electrons into your brain, slightly higher-powered than the rest of the electrons already there, and then mapped all the electrical discharges that occurred. We repeated that process 132 times in quick succession, and now we have a complete map of your brain. We'll have to ask you some questions to make sure it mapped correctly. We'll compare your answers with those given by the digital emulation of your brain.”
“Okay, shoot.”
“What's your favorite color?”
“Light blue.”
“What's your least favorite color?”
“Dark yellow.”
“Spell your name.”
“A-R-T-H-U-R... but technically that's a request, not a question.”
“That's true. I was going to say, 'Spell your name backwards,' but now I'm going to ask, 'How do you spell your name backwards?'”
“R-U-H-T-R-A.”
Jasper asked 500 questions in total, starting with simple questions Arthur could answer immediately, then moving on to logic puzzles with multiple solutions, carefully recording which solution Arthur chose. Finally, he finished with 30 personal questions that required entire stories as answers, such as 'What is your biggest regret?', 'What are you most proud of?', and 'What is your oldest memory?'
“Okay, that was everything. You're free to go now,” Jasper said.
“Oh. When will I know what my emulation said?”
“Well, probably not for another year or two. It'll take us a few weeks just to get your mind emulation onto the servers. We store all the data in a computer that's a little bigger than a one-foot cube. After that, it'll probably take about two years for your emulation to answer all the questions. There shouldn't be any problems, though.”
“That's a long time, but I guess it makes sense for how complicated our minds are.”
“Yeah. I was surprised at first as well.”
“Oh, by the way... are you hiring?”
Arthur had been thinking about it. It definitely seemed more interesting than giving lectures for the next ten years. More importantly, it was a second chance to help change the world.
“Well, we always need more help, but we're a bit short on funding, so it won't be a particularly well-paying job.”
“That's fine. It's not the money I want anyway. It'll be nice to start working in neuroscience again.”
“Great! Welcome to Paradox.”
