Arthur was only 10 when his father was diagnosed with Alzheimer's. He had heard of it from movies and TV shows, but he wasn't aware it was an actual disease. His father, Mark, was still allowed to stay home, but Arthur was told that his father might become a little different over the next few years.
His mother, Rose, was definitely on the wealthier side of the economy, and their house was fairly large. She paid for many treatments to help fight the Alzheimer's, but it was still an incurable disease, no matter how much money she poured into it.
It wasn't until about two years later that it became really bad. Mark was beginning to forget important moments in his life and was starting to act differently.
"Arthur, is that you?" he said as Arthur walked into the room. "You look so much older than the last time I saw you."
Arthur had learned that it wasn't worth explaining that he had visited yesterday. In Mark's mind, it had been months since he could remember seeing Arthur, whose image remained that of a much younger boy.
"Yes, it's me. I promise I'm going to hang out more often, every day if I can."
"I would love that. I've missed you so much these last few months."
"I missed you too. I do have a question. I was hoping you could help answer it for me."
Mark had been a philosopher for 10 years before Arthur was born, and the subject had always interested him long before that. Arthur had picked up many of his father's habits and ideologies, along with his mentality of always being curious.
"I'll do my best."
"So suppose I know someone who has Alzheimer's and is forgetting things. Are they still the same person after a while, or are they a new person?"
"Well, that question is very vague. You could mean their stream of consciousness, which is still the same stream of consciousness it has always been, but their mind is also changing, so they are not the same person in that context. Identity is a very big part of philosophy. You're going to need to be more specific about what you mean."
By the time Arthur had to go home and get to bed, he had many more questions he wanted to ask, but Mark wasn't going to remember this conversation. He would have to do it all again, and he probably wouldn't get much further. More than anything, Arthur wanted to know whether the man with Alzheimer's was still his father.

"We estimate he's going to die within three years. His Alzheimer's is getting much worse. Even our best technology and medication won't keep him around forever."
The doctor delivered the news to Rose while keeping an impressively straight face, although slight twitches suggested it was difficult.
"Are you sure there's truly nothing?" Rose was becoming desperate.
"We do have one option, but it's kind of a long shot."
"What is it?"
"Well, it's an artificial coma. We stimulate and sustain the rest of his body while cooling his brain and reducing the electrical activity within it. It could keep him alive for another twenty years. Our current predictions show that by the time he's close to dying, a cure for Alzheimer's will likely have been created."
"How much will it cost?" Rose sounded as though she had already made up her mind.
"I should warn you, though. Overall, you'll have less than a quarter of the time with him if this fails than you would have had otherwise."
Silence followed. Arthur was already weighing the moral implications of both choices, but he knew he would have to talk with Mark to help sort them out.
◇─◇──◇──◇─◇
Arthur began studying neuroscience, becoming one of the many people working toward a cure for Alzheimer's while also working toward a cure for his father, now in an induced coma.
His motivation carried him far. He earned a master's degree in neurobiology, then got a job at Biogen, where he spent the next five years working on a cure, right up until the final year of his father's artificial coma.
◇─◇──◇──◇─◇
Arthur was there when they woke his father for the final time.
"Hello, Father. You haven't seen me in a long time, but it's me, Arthur."
His father took a little while to regain consciousness, and when he finally did, Arthur repeated himself.
"Father? I don't know anybody named Arthur."
"Your son, Arthur. Please, you have to remember me."
"I don't have a son. What is this? Where am I? Is this some sort of joke?"
"No, look. I have a photo of me when I was little. Do you remember this person?"
Arthur pulled out a photo of himself when he was eight years old and showed it to Mark.
"No, you have the wrong guy. What is this? Is this a test? I don't want to be here. Let me out. Why can't I move? Why does this guy think he's my son?"
◇─◇──◇──◇─◇
Arthur stood at Mark's funeral six months later. He had visited his father often, although nothing ever improved. Mark was always thinking of new explanations for what might be happening to him, although he often forgot the old ones and cycled back through them again.
◇─◇──◇──◇─◇
Over the next two months, Arthur developed a drinking habit. He quit his job, though he had made enough money that he didn't have to sell his house. Grief tore him apart, and he drifted into an identity crisis, as the one purpose that had driven him through life now lay buried six feet underground.
On a Sunday afternoon, he was sitting in his recliner, which had become so worn that he could feel the springs beneath the cushions from how much he sat in it. It was no longer white, but brown, and it smelled of alcohol. His head hurt, and he was only half paying attention to the news.
"Major Japanese pharmaceutical company Eisai has created the first prototype for an Alzheimer's cure. We are here interviewing Jisxin Xein, the scientist who came up with the breakthrough directly responsible for the creation of this drug."
◇─◇──◇──◇─◇
Three months later, his money was running out, and he knew he needed to make a change in his life. He decided to study philosophy. It had always interested him. He took classes and, over the course of a few years, became one of the best in the field.
He did that for the next 30 years. It satisfied him enough, but still left him with the feeling that he had never truly accomplished anything with his life—that he had not changed the world. Other than by the people who were deeply interested in philosophy, he would not be remembered. He had made only a few philosophical breakthroughs, and they were minor at best.
◇─◇──◇──◇─◇
Then, after giving a lecture, he was offered a second chance. Still, it technically wouldn't be his own stream of consciousness that ended up changing the world, but it would still be Arthur. The copy could not exist without the original, and it would not be who it was without the life Arthur had lived and the experiences he had gained.
The world was going to change because of him. His life had not amounted to nothing, even if he died before he could truly do something great, as he knew he would.
